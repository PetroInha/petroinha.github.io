Korea are approaching it from usefully different starting points. This October, CURE Lab
hosts a one-day workshop bringing the two research communities into the same room.

<div class="sec-head">
  <div class="sec-num">01</div>
  <h3>Why Vietnam and Korea on CCS</h3>
</div>

Vietnam committed to net zero by 2050, and carbon capture, utilization and storage is
named explicitly in its National Strategy for Climate Change to 2050. What makes the
Vietnamese case interesting to a Korean research group is the geology behind that
commitment: the country has substantial offshore oil and gas infrastructure, and a
national energy plan that puts real numbers against storage.

<div class="stat-row">
  <div class="stat">
    <p class="stat-num">34</p>
    <p class="stat-cap">offshore oil and gas fields evaluated for CO₂ storage potential</p>
  </div>
  <div class="stat">
    <p class="stat-num">~12 Gt</p>
    <p class="stat-cap">estimated national storage capacity across aquifers, depleted fields and coal beds</p>
  </div>
  <div class="stat">
    <p class="stat-num">1 Mt/yr</p>
    <p class="stat-cap">national capture-capacity target by 2040, rising to 3–6 Mt/yr by 2050</p>
  </div>
</div>

Korea's position is close to the mirror image. The emissions targets are comparably hard,
the engineering capability is well developed — but domestic storage capacity is tightly
constrained, which pushes Korean CCS research toward making the most of difficult
reservoirs and toward partnerships abroad. Two research programs working the same physics
under opposite constraints tend to have a great deal to say to each other.

group, KIGAM and the Korean universities each get to present a coherent body of work
